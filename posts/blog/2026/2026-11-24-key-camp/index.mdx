I am building **Key Camp**, a hands-on AI camp for grades 3–12.

The idea started with a simple observation: AI education should not be limited to students who already want to become software engineers. A portfolio might hold a construction project, an art practice, a community story, a small business idea, or a piece of software. The student’s interests come first.

The first course pathway starts with infrastructure. Students set up a Raspberry Pi, secure it with Tailscale, install OpenClaw, configure a model and communication channel, and then direct their agent to build a personal digital headquarters. The agent does much of the implementation; the student owns the goal, constraints, testing, feedback, and decisions.

Key Camp applies the scientific method to agentic engineering. Students make a claim, test what they built, share it with someone else, use the feedback, and improve the next version. The course is about judgment, not memorizing prompts.

I am especially interested in serving rural Indigenous communities and inner-city youth. I grew up low-income and Native American, and I want students who are often priced out of model subscriptions and advanced technology programs to have a chance to create with these tools.

The long-term goal is bigger than a course. I want to build a teacher-ready education publisher with pathways across creative work, trades, community projects, and computer science. The first step is a useful curriculum that helps one student turn an idea into something real.
